Classifying the 2026 LLM API Market

Companies pay to use AI language models like GPT, Claude and Gemini. There are hundreds to choose from, at very different prices. We asked: which ones are actually good value?

Finding 1

The market has 7 price/quality groups

One group is expensive but only average. You pay more without getting a better model.

Finding 2

A model's group can be guessed from basic facts

Facts like release date and maker give the group away, without looking at price or test scores.

Finding 3

"Best score per dollar" is a bad rule

It just picks the cheapest model. Picking the best model you can afford worked better.

Choosing an AI model is hard because prices vary wildly and keep changing.

A company with a fixed budget can't easily tell which model gives the most for its money. We set out to answer three questions.

Which model should a company pick for its task and budget?

How the four numbers were measured

Models: distinct text-model names in the cleaned price list. Companies: distinct sellers (providers) in that list. Price gap: we split models into ten bands by test score and took the largest most-expensive ÷ cheapest ratio inside one band. Price changes: share of the … models in the weekly price history (Jan 2025 → Sep 2026) whose input or output price changed at least once.

Related work and method

Earlier studies tracked overall AI price trends and built quality-adjusted price indexes (NBER w34608; The Price of Intelligence, 2026), or routed requests to cheaper models (FrugalGPT). They did not sort the market into tiers or turn tiers into a budget rule.

The project follows CRISP-DM: business understanding → data understanding → preparation → modeling → evaluation → deployment. Formal research questions: RQ1 tiers (clustering), RQ2 tier classification, RQ3 model selection (rule-based decision algorithm). Python with pandas, scikit-learn, XGBoost and SHAP.

Three problems showed up straight away:

  • The two datasets spell model names differently, so we had to match them by name.
  • Most models took only a few of the many tests, so no single test covers everyone.
  • Tests use different scales (percent, points, rankings), so their scores can't be compared directly.
More detail

There are 180 distinct benchmarks; the most common covers only 86 results, and the model × test table is only 12.5% filled. The price list also contains image, audio, video and embedding models, and lists the same model once per seller at different prices. All files were loaded into one SQLite database with keys and indexes for profiling.

Data preparation

Cleaning the data

We kept only text models, fixed bad prices, and gave every model one price and one score.

  • Removed image, audio, video and other non-text models, plus duplicate listings.
  • Fixed obvious price typos and filled short gaps in the weekly prices.
  • Matched … of … scored models to their prices. The few left over are different versions, so we left them unmatched rather than guess.
One price

Price per 1 million tokens

A token is roughly ¾ of a word. We mix the input and output price, weighted toward input because most requests send more text than they get back.

One score

Score from 0 to 1

On each test we rank the models: 1 = best, 0 = worst. A model's score is its average rank across the tests it took. This lets us compare tests with different scales.

That left … models with both a price and a score.

How we measured this

Notebook 00 runs … automated checks (… open failures). Text modes kept: chat, responses, completion. Regional copies (e.g. azure/eu/…) dropped; benchmark spelling variants merged (180 → 168). One-week A→B→A price spikes reverted, >10× jumps flagged, internal gaps forward-filled and marked as imputed.

price = (3 · input + 1 · output) / 4, median across sellers (ignores short-lived free or promo listings).
percentile(model, test) = (rank − 1) / (n − 1), only tests taken by ≥ 5 priced models; score = mean percentile.

Modeling & evaluation · Question 1

Finding the groups ✓ Confirmed

…

We gave the computer each model's price and score and let it sort them into groups of similar models (clustering). We tried two different sorting methods; both agreed on 7 groups.

7 groups of models by price and score

Each dot is one model. Further right = more expensive; higher = better test scores. The price axis is stretched so cheap and expensive models both fit. Hover a dot to see the model.

Method details

Features: log₁₀ price and the composite score, standardized. Ward hierarchical clustering chose k = 7 by a vote across silhouette, Davies–Bouldin and Calinski–Harabasz for k = 2–8; K-means with k = 7 gives the final tiers. Stability was checked with the Adjusted Rand Index between the two methods, across 50 K-means seeds and 50 bootstrap 80% subsamples, plus a re-run on better-measured models. "Typical" = median.

What this means. We expected the maker and open-weight status to matter most. They help, but the release date matters more. Part of the reason: newer models score higher partly just because they are compared with other new models.
Method details

Decision Tree vs Random Forest vs XGBoost, nested and repeated 5×10 cross-validation; main metric macro F1 (each group counts equally, since the smallest has 6 models). XGBoost macro F1 … vs … for the majority-class baseline; label-permutation test p ≈ 0.01, so H2a is supported.

Importance by mean |SHAP| (chart) and grouped permutation. H2b required lab or open_weights in the top 3 of both rankings: open_weights is 3rd (SHAP) and 4th (permutation), lab 5th and 8th → partial. Leakage guard: every price and benchmark column removed; with them macro F1 reaches 0.90.

Modeling & evaluation · Question 3

Picking a model ✗ Not confirmed

Our "best score per dollar" rule ended up picking the cheapest model every time in our test.

The picker takes a task, any must-haves, expected usage and a monthly budget. Then it:

  1. Keeps models that can do the task (coding, reasoning or reading images).
  2. Keeps models that meet the must-haves, such as open-weight only or a minimum memory size.
  3. Drops models whose monthly cost would go over budget.
  4. Ranks the rest by score per dollar.
  5. Shows the top 3, with their group.

To test it, we pretended it was December 2025. Each rule picked a model using only what was known then. Then we checked how those picks did in September 2026.

Picking the best model you can afford did best

Average score of the picked model in September 2026, coding tasks (0 if it went over budget or was no longer sold). Blue = our rule; green = the rule found afterwards; grey = simple rules to beat.

What this means. Price differences are so large that dividing score by price lets the cheapest model win. A different rule, "best score that fits the budget", did better than both simple rules. We found it after seeing the results, so it still needs a fair test. The model picker offers both.
Method details

Backtest cutoff 2025-12-29, evaluated at 2026-09-28; coding, lenient mode, … scenarios (usage × budget × requirements). Price = cheapest seller that week, free tiers excluded. Achieved value = task score while staying under budget; over-budget or delisted picks score 0. Only coding has candidates at this cutoff because reasoning and vision tests cover 2026 models only.
