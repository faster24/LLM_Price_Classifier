Classifying the 2026 LLM API Market

What price/performance tiers exist in the LLM API market, and can these tiers help a company pick the best-value model for its task and budget?

CRISP-DM phase 1

Business understanding

Companies that build on LLM APIs must choose from … text models offered by … providers. Models with similar benchmark scores can differ in price by up to …, and prices move: … of models changed price at least once since January 2025. A company with a fixed budget cannot easily tell which model gives the best value for its task.

How these four numbers were measured

N = distinct text-model names in the cleaned pricing catalog. M = distinct providers in that catalog. X = the largest max ÷ min blended price among tiered models in the same performance decile (tenth of the composite score). Y = share of models in the weekly price history (… models, Jan 2025 → Sep 2026, observed weeks only) whose input or output price changed at least once at any provider.

Earlier work measured overall LLM price trends and built quality-adjusted price indexes (NBER w34608; The Price of Intelligence, 2026) or routed requests to cheaper models (FrugalGPT). None of them (a) splits the market into price/performance tiers or (b) turns those tiers into a budget-based selection method. This project does both.

Research questions and hypotheses

RQQuestionHypothesisTechnique
RQ1 TiersWhat price/performance tiers exist in the LLM API market? H1: The market forms at least 3 distinct tiers, including one with high price but no matching benchmark advantage.Clustering
RQ2 Tier classificationCan a model's tier be classified from its features, and which features decide it? H2: A classifier assigns tiers better than a majority-class baseline, and non-performance features (provider, closed status) rank among the top deciding features.Classification
RQ3 Model selectionWhich model should a company choose for a given task, requirements and budget, now and in the near future? H3: In a backtest, models picked by the decision algorithm give better value than the rules "pick the cheapest" and "pick the top benchmark."Rule-based decision algorithm

The three questions form one pipeline: clustering creates the tiers, classification explains them, and a decision algorithm uses them to recommend a model.

Data understanding

What

Two public Kaggle datasets by the same author: one about prices, one about benchmarks.

How

Why

Price alone cannot show value, and benchmarks alone cannot show cost. We need both, linked per model.

What we found while profiling

  • No shared key. The two datasets name models differently (anthropic/claude-sonnet-5-5 vs us.anthropic.claude-sonnet-5-5), so they must be joined by normalized names.
  • Sparse benchmarks. 180 distinct benchmarks, but the most common covers only 86 rows. The model × test matrix is only 12.5% filled.
  • Mixed scales. Scores come as %, Elo or index points, so raw scores can never be compared across benchmarks.
  • Not all are text LLMs. The catalog also lists image, embedding, audio and video models.
  • One model, many prices. The same model appears once per provider, at different prices.
CRISP-DM phase 3

Data preparation

What

Validate and clean every table, then build one price number and one performance number per model.

How

Notebook 00 runs … automated checks (… open failures) and writes the clean files used by every later step.

Why

Clustering and classification are only as good as their inputs. Bad prices or mixed scales would create fake tiers.

Raw → clean

  • Text LLMs only: modes chat, responses, completion; image, video, audio, embedding and guard models dropped.
  • Duplicates: regional copies (e.g. azure/eu/…) dropped; benchmark spelling variants merged (180 → 168 benchmarks); best score kept per model and test.
  • Price history: one-week A→B→A spikes reverted, >10× jumps flagged, internal gaps forward-filled and marked as imputed.
  • Join: normalized names link … of … benchmarked models to their price series. The unmatched ones are genuinely different builds (previews, dated variants), so they stay visible instead of being force-matched.

One price, one performance score

Price = the 3 : 1 blended price, median across providers, in USD per 1M tokens. Typical workloads send about three times more input than output. The median ignores temporary free or promo listings.

Performance = for each test covered by ≥ 5 priced models, rank the models and scale the rank to 0 (worst) … 1 (best); a model's score is the mean of its percentiles. This puts %, Elo and index scores on one scale and works even though each model has a different set of tests.

Formulas

blended = (3 · input + 1 · output) / 4
percentile(model, test) = (rank − 1) / (n_test − 1)
performance(model) = mean over its tests of percentile

Result: … priced text LLMs with a performance score. This is the analysis set for RQ1 and RQ2.

CRISP-DM phase 4

Modeling

RQ1 · Clustering: finding the tiers

What

Group the models on two features: log price and performance.

How

Ward hierarchical clustering picks k by a vote across silhouette, Davies–Bouldin and Calinski–Harabasz (k = 2–8). K-means with that k gives the final tiers.

Why

Two methods with different assumptions: if both agree on k and on memberships, the tiers are not an artifact of one algorithm. Log price, because prices span three orders of magnitude.

Stability checks

Adjusted Rand Index between Ward and K-means, across 50 K-means seeds, and across 50 bootstrap 80% subsamples. A sensitivity re-run on better-measured models (more tests per model) checks that the tiers do not depend on models scored on a single test.

RQ2 · Classification: what decides a tier?

What

Predict a model's tier from features that are not price or benchmark scores.

How

Decision Tree vs Random Forest vs XGBoost, nested and repeated 5×10 cross-validation, macro F1. Importance by SHAP and by grouped permutation.

Why

The tree is readable, the forest is robust, boosting is usually strongest: comparing them shows the price of interpretability. Macro F1 because tiers are imbalanced (smallest has 6 models).

Leakage guard. The tiers were built from price and performance, so every price and benchmark column is removed. Otherwise the classifier would just re-learn the clustering (with them, macro F1 reaches 0.90).

Features fall into three groups: Vendor (lab, open weights, number of providers), Capability (context, max output, modalities, reasoning, tools…), Time (release date).

Mean |SHAP| per feature for the XGBoost classifier, colored by feature group. Hover for the permutation rank too.

RQ3 · Decision algorithm: picking a model

What

A filter-then-rank rule that returns the top 3 models, with their RQ1 tier, for a task, requirements, monthly usage and budget.

How

Hard filters first, then a ranking by value. Price = cheapest provider at that week; free tiers excluded.

Why

Rules are transparent and auditable: a company can see why a model was dropped. Filters encode must-haves; ranking encodes preference.

  1. Task: keep models with the needed capability (coding, reasoning, vision input, function calling, JSON output).
  2. Requirements: minimum context, open-weight or any, include/exclude providers, minimum task score, released within N months.
  3. Budget: expected input/output tokens × price ≤ monthly budget.
  4. Rank by value score = task benchmark score ÷ monthly cost.
  5. Return the top 3 models with their tier.

Task → benchmark mapping, chosen by coverage: coding → SWE-Bench, Terminal-Bench, Aider Polyglot, SciCode, LiveCodeBench; reasoning → Humanity's Last Exam, GPQA Diamond, AIME; vision → MMMU Pro, MathVision.

H2a ✓ XGBoost reaches macro F1 … against … for the majority-class rule, and a label-permutation test gives p ≈ 0.01, so the features carry real information about the tier.
H2b ~ open_weights ranks 3rd in SHAP but 4th in permutation importance; lab ranks 5th and 8th. The pre-registered rule needed top 3 in both rankings, so this is partial.

What we learned. Release date is the strongest feature in both rankings. This points to an era bias in RQ1: older models are scored against older peers, so "when it was released" partly stands in for "how it performs". A fix to try is restricting the analysis to recent models.

H3 · Model selection ✗ Not supported

Backtest: decide with the data available at 29 Dec 2025 (models, prices and scores up to that week only), then judge each pick at 28 Sep 2026: the task score it delivers while staying under budget. A pick that went over budget or is no longer sold scores 0. Primary setting: coding, … scenarios (usage × budget × requirements).

Mean achieved score in 2026 (0–1) per method, coding. Blue = the proposed algorithm; grey = baselines and reference rules. Hover for the failure rate.

What we learned. Score ÷ cost is dominated by cost: the cheapest model wins almost every time, so the algorithm picks exactly what "pick the cheapest" picks. It never beats "pick the top benchmark" on average.

Exploratory, not confirmed: a budget-aware score-first rule (best score that fits the budget) beats both baselines. Because it was found after seeing the results, it needs a pre-registered test before it can be claimed. The live recommender offers both rules.
Limitations. 144 models is a small sample; most performance scores rest on 1–3 tests; only coding has candidates at the Dec 2025 cutoff, because reasoning and vision benchmarks cover 2026 models only.
